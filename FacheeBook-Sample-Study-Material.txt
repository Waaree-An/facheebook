1. WHY REREADING IS NOT ENOUGH
============================================================

Rereading can make information feel familiar. Familiarity is useful, but it does not always mean that you can remember an idea later or explain it without looking.

A stronger check is to close the book and try to bring the main ideas to mind. This is called active recall, or retrieval practice. After trying, check your notes and correct what you missed.

Example:

1. Read a short section about the water cycle.
2. Close the book.
3. Write or say the main stages from memory.
4. Open the book and check your answer.
5. Correct any missing or mistaken steps.

The effort to remember is part of the practice. Getting an answer wrong is useful when you notice the mistake, check the correct idea, and try again later.

============================================================
2. WHAT IS SPACED PRACTICE?
============================================================

Spaced practice means reviewing material more than once, with time between reviews. Instead of trying to learn everything in one long session, study a little, take a break, and return to it later.

A simple example plan:

- First study: learn the idea and make a short note.
- Later the same day: try to recall the idea without looking.
- The next day: answer a few questions about it.
- A few days later: review the parts that were difficult.
- The next week: explain the idea again in your own words.

This is a sample plan, not a strict rule. The best review times depend on the subject, the exam date, and how well you remember the material.

3. A SHORT STUDY ROUTINE
============================================================

Try this routine for one small topic:

STEP 1 — CHOOSE
Choose one topic or a few pages. Make the task small enough to finish.

STEP 2 — UNDERSTAND
Read carefully. Look up unfamiliar words and write down the main idea.

STEP 3 — RECALL
Close your material. Write, draw, or say what you remember.

STEP 4 — CHECK
Compare your recall with the original material. Correct errors in a different color or with a clear note.

STEP 5 — PLAN A RETURN
Choose a time to review the topic again. Put that review in your study plan or calendar.

STEP 6 — TAKE A BREAK
Take a short break before beginning a new topic. A timer can help you keep the session and break clear.

4. HOW TO MAKE A USEFUL FLASHCARD
============================================================

A useful flashcard asks one clear question and has a short answer.

Less useful:
Front: Explain all of biology.
Back: A long chapter copied from a book.

More useful:
Front: What does active recall ask a learner to do?
Back: Try to remember or explain information without looking, then check and correct the answer.

Make cards from ideas you need to remember. Do not copy every sentence. If a card feels too difficult, split it into smaller questions.

5. COMMON STUDY PROBLEMS AND HELPFUL CHOICES
============================================================

PROBLEM: "I read the page, but I cannot explain it."
TRY: Read a smaller section, close the book, and explain its main idea in simple words.

PROBLEM: "I only study just before an exam."
TRY: Make a small review plan and return to important topics on different days.

PROBLEM: "I keep forgetting my mistakes."
TRY: Keep a short mistake list. Write the correct idea and revisit it during your next review.

PROBLEM: "My phone distracts me."
TRY: Choose one small task, silence non-urgent alerts if safe to do so, and keep only the material you need open.

PROBLEM: "My study plan feels too large."
TRY: Choose one task that can be completed in a short session. Starting small is better than waiting for a perfect plan.

============================================================
6. KEY TAKEAWAYS
============================================================

1. Feeling familiar with a page is not the same as recalling its ideas.
2. Active recall means trying to remember before checking the material.
3. Check and correct mistakes after recall.
4. Spaced practice means returning to material over time instead of doing all review at once.
5. Short, focused questions make useful flashcards.
6. A small, clear study task can make it easier to begin.

7. PRACTICE QUESTIONS
============================================================

Try to answer before looking at the answer key.

QUESTION 1
What should you do during active recall?

QUESTION 2
Why should you check your notes after trying to remember?

QUESTION 3
What does spaced practice mean?

QUESTION 4 — TRUE OR FALSE
Copying a full page onto a flashcard usually makes the card easier to review.

QUESTION 5
Name one way to make a study task feel smaller.

ANSWER KEY

1. Try to remember or explain the idea without looking at the material.
2. To find and correct missing or mistaken information.
3. Return to material in more than one study session, with time between reviews.
4. False. A short, focused question and answer is usually easier to use.
5. For example, choose one topic, a few pages, or one short study session.

============================================================
8. A ONE-WEEK PRACTICE PLAN
============================================================

DAY 1
Study one short topic. Write three questions about it.

DAY 2
Answer the questions without looking. Check your notes and correct mistakes.

DAY 3
Review only the questions that were difficult.

DAY 4
Explain the topic aloud or write a short explanation from memory.

DAY 5
Check the explanation against your material. Add one useful flashcard if needed.

DAY 6
Take a break from this topic or study a different one.

DAY 7
Try the three questions again and decide what needs more review.

Make the plan shorter or longer to fit your real schedule. Rest, meals, health, family responsibilities, and school deadlines matter too.
